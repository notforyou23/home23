## Initial editorial judgment

Before broad research, the piece had two provisional ideas:

1. **“Reuben can lie, but his mandolin cannot.”** The instrument exposes the betrayal before Reuben admits it.
2. A song that feels deeply native to Garcia and the Dead was barely a Grateful Dead song at all.

These were hypotheses, not conclusions.

## Discovery behavior

The run did not rely on one synthetic dossier. It used repeated, targeted searches and page reads to close individual claims.

The important move was following mismatches:
- Garcia’s recorded ending versus Hunter’s extra verses
- official title variants
- Garcia repertoire versus Grateful Dead repertoire
- thematic Dead-ness versus actual performance scarcity

Those mismatches generated the article’s deeper turns.

## Interpretive synthesis

The evidence produced a layered argument:

1. Reuben’s betrayal occurs in musical tone before overt action.
2. The mandolin becomes witness, judge, and involuntary confession.
3. Ruby is constructed as Cherise’s red double.
4. Hunter’s omitted verses reveal that the ending is an underworld retrieval.
5. Garcia’s interview confirms the Orpheus / *Black Orpheus* lineage.
6. The song’s own discarded-and-rewritten melody mirrors its resurrection theme.
7. Its performance history establishes it as a Garcia song the Dead only briefly inhabited.
8. The oath and the “unsung song” line complete the argument: truth emerges through art when the player will not speak it.
